The Benefits and Drawbacks of IRAs

May 26, 2026

Individual retirement accounts come in two main flavors traditional and Roth and each offers a different tax trade-off. Traditional IRAs typically give you a tax deduction in the year you contribute, with withdrawals taxed as ordinary income later. Roth IRAs work in reverse: no upfront deduction, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

IRAs also tend to offer far more investment choice than a typical employer retirement plan, where your options are usually limited to a short list of funds. With an IRA, you generally have access to a much broader range of investments to match your strategy.

Roth IRAs in particular offer more flexibility than people expect direct contributions can usually be withdrawn early without taxes or penalties, which isn't the case for most retirement accounts.

On the drawback side, annual contribution limits for IRAs are lower than many workplace plans, and early withdrawals from a traditional IRA generally come with both taxes and a penalty, with a few narrow exceptions. Traditional IRAs also require minimum withdrawals starting at a certain age, which limits how long your money can keep compounding tax-deferred a rule that doesn't apply to Roth IRAs during your lifetime.

Used thoughtfully contributing consistently and investing only money you won't need before retirement an IRA remains one of the most effective tools available for building long-term wealth.

A Roth conversion means moving money from a traditional IRA into a Roth IRA. You may owe taxes in the year you convert, but qualified withdrawals from a Roth IRA afterward are tax-free — which can make conversions especially powerful in the right circumstances.

One common scenario is converting during an unusually low-income year. If your tax bracket is temporarily lower than usual — say, during a career break or a year of graduate school — paying taxes on the conversion now, while your rate is low, can be far cheaper than paying ordinary income tax on withdrawals down the road.

Another scenario is the "backdoor Roth": if you earn too much to contribute to a Roth IRA directly, you can still make a non-deductible contribution to a traditional IRA and then convert it. Done correctly, this lets you capture the same long-term tax-free growth as a direct Roth contribution.

Conversions aren't right for everyone. If you expect to need the converted funds within the next five years, or you don't have separate cash on hand to cover the resulting tax bill, it's usually better to leave the account as-is. As with most tax decisions, it's worth thinking through your specific timeline before converting.
